## Why LLM Systems need Multi-Agent Principles

The frontier of Large language models (LLMs) has moved beyond chatbots and individual models to [systems composed of multiple LLMs](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2024/02/18/compound-ai-systems/) (each with potentially different skills, latency profiles, price points, and capabilities) working together on complex multi-step tasks. Such systems power emerging tools like Google’s [Vertex AI](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/build-and-manage-multi-system-agents-with-vertex-ai), [Perplexity Labs’](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/introducing-perplexity-labs) multi-agent orchestration, OpenAI’s [deep research assistant](https://openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research/), and Anthropic’s [computer use](https://www.anthropic.com/news/3-5-models-and-computer-use), where multiple LLMs, search, and tool calls run in parallel or interleave through multiple steps of reasoning.

Yet without thoughtful multi-agent design  principles, these systems may struggle in the wild. Tasks might stall if a single model blocks the pipeline. One agent might take an action that is unsuitable for another agent later in the workflow. Downstream modules might wait unnecessarily on dependent tasks instead of starting independent ones. To overcome these and other challenges in a principled way, models must not only generate good outputs and make good decisions individually. They must also coordinate their reasoning and actions with each other. Such coordination problems are ones the multi-agent systems (MAS) and multi-agent reinforcement learning communities have long grappled with and that [multi-LLM systems are equally vulnerable to](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21298).

The shift toward agentic training methods like [Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards](https://limit-of-rlvr.github.io/) (RLVR), an active area of research, will only increase the complexity of multi-LLM systems. Models like OpenAI’s O3, xAI’s Grok-4, and Deepseek R1 tend to focus on domains like math and code where output correctness is easily verifiable. Such a focus creates increasingly specialized models, each better at different tasks. The upshot is that they become more uneven and harder to slot into general-purpose workflows. For instance, OpenAI maintains distinct versions of O3 for standard chat and “deep research” because of these capability tradeoffs. As agent specialization grows, so does the need for systems that can coordinate diverse agents.

The need for scalable and robust multi-agent coordination leads to important computational tradeoffs with another desirable property of multi-LLM systems, i.e., supporting anytimeness or being interruptible in a principled way. In many complex and multi-step applications—from deep research to automated coding to long-form content generation—users often want meaningful partial answers before full completion. Wouldn’t it be great if we could ask the deep research agent to give a current meaningful summary of its progress and findings after a few minutes, while it continues to research the topic and develop the report over many hours?

## Our Idea: MaxPlus with Search

In this post, we explore how ideas from the multi-agent decision-making literature—specifically, our prior work [Scalable Online Planning for Multi-Agent MDPs](https://www.jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/13261) (Best Paper at AAMAS 2021, Journal of AI Research 2022)—can be valuable for modern multi-LLM systems. The LLM agent space is moving at breakneck speed; our goal was to identify some enduring and evergreen principles and to paint a picture of how well-established coordination algorithms can help address today’s most pressing challenges in building scalable, interruptible, and cost-effective multi-LLM applications.

The core idea of our paper was to prioritize online scaling, i.e., inference-time efficient scaling, in sequential multi-agent decision settings. It combined two key pillars:

- Message Passing on a Coordination Graph: Using the [MaxPlus algorithm](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/11780519_1), each agent shares messages with its neighbors to efficiently identify high-value joint actions without enumerating the exponentially large joint action space. Because Max-Plus is iterative, it yields usable solutions even if stopped early, making it ideal for real-time applications.
- Monte-Carlo Tree Search: MCTS strategically allocates simulation effort to likely states and continuously improves value estimates. We integrated Max-Plus at each tree node, maintaining the flexibility and anytime performance of MCTS as it scales to handle dynamic scenarios involving many agents.

Though our numerical experiments were on simplified settings, they showed how we could match or outperform exact, i.e., non-anytime baseline methods while running significantly faster and handling instances that baselines could not manage due to memory constraints. The key insight is that integrating factorized coordination and iterative message passing into tree search enables scalable and anytime multi-agent coordination in sequential decision-making scenarios.

## Mathematical Foundation

The Max-Plus coordination algorithm optimizes a distributed utility function:

$$
U(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i(x_i, \{x_j : j \in N_i\})
$$

Where $x_i$ represents the instruction for agent $i$, and $N_i$ denotes the dynamic neighborhood determined by the orchestrator.

The message-passing updates follow:

$$
m_{i \to j}^{(t+1)} = \max_{x_i} \left[ u_i(x_i, \mathbf{x}_{N_i \setminus j}) + \sum_{k \in N_i \setminus j} m_{k \to i}^{(t)} \right]
$$

## Key Implications for Multi-LLM Systems

1. Anytime Responses: The Max-Plus/MCTS combination enables a useful partial solution at every step. Even with a limited execution window, the system can provide coherent intermediate outputs essential for real-time interfaces and latency-sensitive applications.
2. Decentralized Parallelism: Local message exchanges between agents yields parallel processing of instruction refinements or sub-problem solving. Tree search manages these concurrent "local conversations," allowing several LLM calls or retrieval operations to execute simultaneously, essential for complex drafting and research use cases.
3. Dynamic, Contextual Coordination: The orchestrator can continually update the coordination graph, i.e., who communicates with whom, based on current intermediate results in a principled way. This dynamic rewiring prioritizes resources effectively and offers greater flexibility compared to static routing methods.
4. Interleaving reasoning with coordination. Multi-agent tree search can allow for branching some thoughts into parallel specialists, wait (or not) for their replies, fold results back in, and continue thinking. That process—reason a bit → coordinate → reason further—mirrors how human teams write papers, debug code, or draft reports.
5. Explicit Trade-offs Between Quality and Cost: Parameters such as tree depth, agent count, neighbor limits, and batch size allow for more transparently balancing quality against resource expenditure.
6. Structuring Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning: Reinforcement learning over multi-LLM workflows faces challenges with credit assignment across multiple interactions and steps. Factored coordination graphs and Max-Plus tries to address this by propagating utilities along the coordination graph, providing a strong planning prior that supports scalable multi-agent reinforcement learning methods.
7. Online Routing, Mixture-of-Experts-Style: Unlike static mixture-of-experts methods, our approach could dynamically route queries, refining or dropping specialists as needed during interactions. This adaptive process optimizes the conversation continuously within explicit budget constraints.

Collectively, these principles and practices can lead to a structured, scalable, and resilient multi-LLM architecture, suitable for both prototype development and production-scale deployments.

## Experiment 1: Multi-Agent Instruction Tuning

This section presents illustrative experiments that demonstrate key principles and ideas from our paper in using multiple agents to tune instructions for a base LLM, i.e., improve its performance on a task solely by optimizing the instruction prompt. Our goal is not to develop an optimal approach for this problem, but rather to highlight the mechanics and potential benefits of dynamic coordination graphs in easily interpretable settings. 

### Multi-LLM Setup

There are three kinds of agents here in order of increasing complexity. We did this to highlight how our ideas can work in a heterogeneous systems with different tradeoffs:

- **Base LLM (gpt-3.5/haiku3)** The frozen LLM whose instructions need to be optimized. A bonus of setting this to the cheapest/least capable models is that the benchmarks are not already saturated.
- **Proposal Agent (gpt4o-mini)** Mid-level agents that will generate the candidate instructions. The number of such agents is a key system parameter that can balance between more diversity and more computation/communication complexity. We use 5 agents for all our experiments.
- **Orchestrator (gpt4o)** Coordinates the proposal agents and gives feedback to improve instructions.

### Algorithm Details

Proposal agents iteratively generate and refine instructions. For each proposed instruction, the evaluator E provides a noisy but inexpensive utility signal by evaluating the base LLM with those instructions on randomly chosen minibatches (default size: 50) from a validation set. Then, the orchestrator (Ω) decides:

- Which agent pairs should exchange information. We set the maximum number of neighbors of any agent in the coordination graph to 2; this parameter controls the coordination complexity.
- What type of feedback (natural-language hints or critiques) each agent receives.


The iterative refinement process follows this structure:

```python
for round in range(N_ROUNDS):
    Ω.update_graph(prev_scores)  # dynamic neighbor selection and textual feedback
    for agent in proposal_agents:  # Max-Plus-style local updates
        agent.refine_instruction(
            neighbor_instructions=instructions_of(Ω.neighbours[agent]),
            feedback=Ω.feedback[agent]
        )
    E.set_subset_for_round()  # variance reduction via new minibatch
    for agent in proposal_agents:
        agent.current_score = E.evaluate_instruction(agent.current_instruction)
```

### Key Concepts Illustrated

- **Dynamic Coordination Graph:** The orchestrator continuously updates interaction patterns (edges), keeping coordination efficient without exploding computational costs. Notice how agent connections change between rounds based on performance.

- **Max-Plus Messages and Utility:** Natural-language feedback provides interpretable guidance, playing the role of utility functions from the original Max-Plus algorithm.

- **Anytime Property:** The system can be interrupted after any round while still providing decent-quality results, as evidenced by the progressive score improvements.

The appendix has a partial output trace of this setup on one simple example of instruction tuning for mathematical reasoning problems. It focuses on the feedback and shows the improvement after each round.

### Evaluation Setup

After each round, the current top-performing prompt is re-evaluated on a held-out test set. We use three datasets for this evaluation:  [GSM8k](https://dspy.ai/deep-dive/data-handling/built-in-datasets/), and two datasets from the [MetaPrompting](https://github.com/suzgunmirac/meta-prompting) paper, Multi-step arithmetic and Geometric Shapes. We compare our approach against three baselines:

- **Vanilla:** Base LLM without prompt optimization.
- **MIPRO-v2** [DSPy’s inbuilt optimizer](https://dspy.ai/api/optimizers/MIPROv2/) for instruction and few-shot example tuning. We use the light mode, which is most comparable to our approach in terms of total computation and token budget.
- **Chain of Thought (CoT):** Standard zero-shot reasoning prompt. _We had trouble getting [dspy.ChainOfThought](https://dspy.ai/api/modules/ChainOfThought/) to parse properly_–we were getting worse results than vanilla for all tasks–so we report numbers from other papers to still enable some comparison.

### Practical Insights/Connections

Recent works, such as [MultiPrompter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16730), [Mixture-of-Expert Prompts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00256), and [HMAW](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20252), also explore multi-agent or multi-prompt optimization scenarios. MultiPrompter employs reinforcement learning for cooperative prompt construction; Mixture-of-Expert Prompts uses clustering for assigning specialized prompt experts; HMAW uses a fixed hierarchy of LLM roles (CEO → Manager → Worker) to generate and then apply a single refined prompt in a one-shot, zero‐training pipeline. Our approach differs by having an explicit coordination graph that evolves based on intermediate outcomes, providing a highly flexible and scalable structure suitable for real-time and adaptive applications. Of course, both the prompt tuning and multi-LLM spaces are evolving quickly, so an exhaustive and up-to-date related work review is out of scope here.


Dynamic and decentralized coordination is critical for real-world production settings. For instance, in complex longform applications like Deep Research or [narratives](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02603), specialized section agents can exchange succinct context summaries, blockers, and critical questions, enabling the orchestrator to make quick decisions without parsing extensive documentation. Similarly, in software development tasks where precision, correctness, and consistency are crucial, dynamically coordinated LLM-based agents can maintain global consistency by rapidly communicating partial results and relevant contextual feedback.

## Experiment 2: Adaptive Divide-and-Conquer Arithmetic

In the second experiment, we explore interleaving dynamic decomposition and coordination in multi-step computational tasks. We focus on arithmetic expressions as a simplified yet clear analogue of complex multi-LLM production tasks, which have clear intermediate and final states, and require balancing efficiency and quality.

Given a multi-step arithmetic expression, e.g., ((1 + 0 + 2 - 4) + (-9 + 6 * -5 + 8)),  the orchestrator dynamically decomposes the computations and delegates them to specialized sub-problem agents to solve. In doing so, our workflow demonstrates adaptive task decomposition, visible intermediate progress, and anytime interruptibility (again simulated through the rounds).

### Multi-LLM Setup

This example uses two kinds of LLMs, one more expensive and the other cheaper and token-constrained. The former coordinates computation without performing arithmetic itself:

- **Decomposer (o3-mini)** identifies binary sub-expressions for parallel evaluation and returns a precise, structured substitution plan using placeholders.
- **Sub-problem agents (gpt-4o-mini-250)** are intentionally constrained to single-operator expressions, reflecting the real-world pattern of leveraging specialized, token-efficient agents for routine subtasks.
- **Substitutor (o3-mini)** systematically merges sub-solver results back into the main expression following the structured substitution plan, clearly indicating whether further decomposition is needed.


### How it works

Given the original problem, the decomposer, sub-solvers, and substitutor work together to make progress towards the solution:

```python
while not done:
    sub_exprs, plan = decomposer.decompose(expr)  # structured placeholder-driven plan
    partial_results = [SubproblemAgent(i).compute(s) for i, s in enumerate(sub_exprs)]
    expr, done = substitutor.substitute(expr, plan, partial_results)
    log(expr)  # clearly demonstrates incremental progress at each step
```

### Evaluation Setup

Compared to the first set on prompt optimization, this set of experiments is more illustrative and qualitative rather than quantitatively detailed. We want our approach to demonstrate several desirable properties–dynamic task decomposition, scalable coordination, anytimeness–without sacrificing solution quality and with potentially better operational cost than a single reasoning agent without any multi-agent principles.

We constructed a dataset of 200 long arithmetic problems by composing pairs of multi-step arithmetic expressions sourced from the MetaPrompting dataset. Our approach involves the orchestrator (GPT-o3-mini) managing task decomposition and substitution, and multiple sub-problem agents (GPT-4o-mini-250) performing minimal arithmetic computations. Here’s one example, with the intermediate results after each round:

_Problem_

((1 + 0 + 2 - 4) + (-9 + 6 * -5 + 8)) = -32

_Answers after round_

**Round 0:** 1 - 2 - 9 - 30 + 8

**Round 1:** 1 - 11 - 22

**Final:** -32

The baseline here is to just solve each expression end-to-end using GPT-o3-mini alone, after which we can compare the total effective token count/cost for either approach. For the above example (full trace in the appendix), we find that the baseline reasoning trace uses 142 o3-mini tokens, while our approach uses 124 o3-mini and 45 4o-mini tokens. At the time of writing, each o3-mini token costs 7x that of 4o-mini, which makes the effective cost of our approach 124 + 45//7 ~ 130.6 o3-mini tokens (compared to 142), a savings of  ~8%.

As you can see from the trace, the token count for our approach makes some generous assumptions about what inter-agent communication is essential and what we can make implicit through dspy type signatures and enforcement. But there are two caveats to this caveat! First, we did not go out of our way to optimize inter-agent communications for these examples. Second, and more importantly, the room for improvement in the token efficiency advantage is only likely to increase as one moves to larger-scale, production-grade tasks.


Both methods correctly solved all the 200 arithmetic problems we gave them, so there was no drop in quality with our approach. While we did not perform exhaustive quantitative token comparisons, a few spot checks indicated that the total effective token cost of our approach, like the example in the appendix, remained competitive and slightly lower (with our assumptions) than that of the single o3-mini reasoning agent.


## Practical Insights/Connections

Our approach relates to several recent and more rigorously evaluated methods that also address dynamic task decomposition and multi-agent interactions. [Atom of Thoughts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12018) focuses on simplifying test-time reasoning by decomposing complex questions into independent atomic subquestions with Markovian properties, optimizing reasoning efficiency by avoiding accumulated historical information. [Forest-of-Thought](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09078) leverages multiple reasoning paths and dynamic self-correction to improve accuracy and efficiency in solving complex logical problems. [Magentic-One](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04468) uses a multi-agent architecture with an orchestrator dynamically coordinating specialized agents to handle diverse tasks, highlighting flexibility in agent collaboration without additional tuning or training.

Our illustration is focused on explicitly integrating dynamic coordination graphs with anytime interruptibility, drawing heavily from our original paper. It combines adaptive spawning of specialized agents and dynamic task decomposition, showing clear intermediate progress interruptibility. These distinctive features are particularly relevant in current real-world multi-LLM applications. In long-form document generation and complex deep research tasks, balancing token budgets and cost while ensuring high-quality output is crucial. In computer operator AI agent systems, task decomposition, specialized agent spawning, and interruptibility can significantly enhance the robustness, responsiveness, and overall efficiency of distributed task execution.


## Why this all matters

Building practical and effective multi-LLM systems will likely require drawing on the [extensive body of knowledge](https://www.masfoundations.org/) developed over decades by the multi-agent planning, coordination, and reinforcement learning communities. While much of this prior work was validated on simplified agent settings, our illustrative experiments suggest that these core ideas—in our case, scalable anytime coordination of heterogeneous teams—can translate meaningfully when applied to more capable LLM-based agents. By adapting our own max-plus orchestration approach, we showed one way these techniques can support more flexible, efficient, and interruptible multi-LLM workflows. Of course, the key challenge lies in carefully adapting these ideas and others from the multi-agent systems community for production-grade domains like research writing, software development, or OS-level automation, where the stakes are higher, and where thoughtful design choices will determine their practical value.
